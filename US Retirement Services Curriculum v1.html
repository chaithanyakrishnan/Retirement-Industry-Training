How to read the categories: DC and DB describe the benefit model; IRA describes an individual arrangement; 401(a) is a qualification framework. MEP, PEP and DCG describe participation or reporting structures, not peer investment products. More than one label can apply to the same arrangement.

Read the category, then the actual plan. This is a learning comparison, not a complete legal classification. ERISA coverage, contribution limits, permitted loans/distributions and rollover treatment vary. Check current primary guidance and the governing arrangement before processing.

Worked distinction

Two employees each see “$50,000.” In a DC plan this may be a valued investment account. In a cash balance DB plan it may be a hypothetical account representing a benefit promise. The same displayed number does not establish the same investment rights, funding model or payment calculation.

Check your understanding: what should you ask first?

Identify the arrangement and governing provisions before interpreting the amount. Then check what the display represents, the participant’s facts, ownership and distribution conditions. Do not infer the plan type from a website’s account layout.

Put it all together

Capstone: one participant, one connected journey

Scenario: Northstar Manufacturing offers a fictional 401(k) plan. Maya enters on 1 July under the example eligibility rule. Her eligible pay for the first payroll is $2,000. She elects 6%, and the employer matches 50% of deferrals up to 6% of pay. She directs new money 60% to Fund A and 40% to Fund B. Ignore fees and annual limits for this exercise only.

Enroll Maya→Calculate money in→Validate file and cash→Allocate investments→Resolve a break→Explain the outcome
  1. Calculate employee deferral, employer match and total contribution.
  2. Calculate amounts directed to each fund. Keep source records even if amounts are invested together.
  3. The payroll file contains $180 for Maya, but only $170 of related cash arrives. List your next steps.
  4. Later, Maya changes future allocation to 100% Fund B. Explain what happens to existing Fund A holdings.
  5. Maya later requests a loan. List facts to check, without inventing a universal loan limit.
  6. At termination, explain why account balance, vested balance and distributable amount may differ.
  7. Identify at least four controls and the evidence each creates.
Facilitator answer guide

1. $120 employee + $60 match = $180. 2. Fund A $108 and Fund B $72; source-level records must remain distinguishable. 3. Record the $10 break, compare receipts and files, check identifiers and timing, contact the accountable team, follow the approved hold/exception process and reconcile before closure. Do not fabricate cash or silently reduce the contribution. 4. Future money goes to B under the new effective instruction; existing A holdings do not automatically move. 5. Check whether the plan permits loans, participant eligibility, available amount, outstanding loans, terms, documentation, consent and repayment arrangements. 6. Vesting concerns ownership; distribution concerns eligibility and timing; pending items and restrictions may also matter. 7. Possible controls: identity validation, duplicate detection, file/cash reconciliation, participant/source reconciliation, approval evidence, access logs and payment-destination verification.

DB orientation: the important difference

In DC, account records are central. In DB, a promised benefit formula is central. A purely fictional formula of 1% × $60,000 final average pay × 20 years of credited service gives $12,000 annual benefit before any relevant commencement-age, payment-option or other adjustments. Real formulas, service definitions, limits and elections require plan-specific review. Do not use this example to calculate an actual entitlement.

Pay + service history→Plan benefit formula→Retirement date and options→Review and election→Pension payroll

Plain-language glossary

Account balance
The value recorded in an account at a stated time.
Beneficiary
A person or entity designated to receive benefits, subject to applicable rules.
Census data
Employee information used to administer the plan.
Compensation
Pay included under the relevant definition for a calculation.
Contribution source
A category identifying where money came from and the rules attached to it.
Deferral
Pay an employee elects to contribute under the arrangement.
Distribution
Money or assets paid out of a plan.
ERISA
A federal law establishing important standards for many private-sector benefit plans.
Forfeiture
A nonvested amount that becomes subject to the plan’s forfeiture rules.
Good order
A request has the information and approvals required to process it.
Match
An employer contribution linked to employee contributions under a formula.
Plan document
The governing written terms of the plan, together with applicable amendments.
QDRO
A qualified domestic relations order meeting applicable requirements.
Reconciliation
Comparing records and explaining or resolving differences.
Rollover
A movement of eligible retirement money to another eligible arrangement under applicable rules.
Source balance
The amount belonging to a particular contribution category.
True-up
A later adjustment under the plan’s calculation rules, often to align a match with the intended period.
Vested balance
The portion owned by the participant under applicable vesting rules.
Workflow
The sequence of work, decisions and handoffs.
Exception
A condition that needs investigation or special handling rather than normal processing.
